# Lecture 03 - The Normal Distribution

## Notes

**Shape.** The normal distribution is the symmetric, single-peaked "bell" curve. It is centered on its mean, and because it is symmetric, mean and median coincide — a contrast with the skewed data from [[Lecture 01 - Descriptive Statistics]].

**Why it matters.** Many measurements pile up around a central value with fewer cases toward the extremes (lecture's examples: heights in a population, repeated careful measurements of the same quantity). The bell shape is a useful model for that pattern.

**The spread is everything.** Two normal curves can share a mean but differ in standard deviation: a small standard deviation gives a tall, narrow bell (values cluster tightly), a large one gives a low, wide bell (values spread out).

**The empirical rule (descriptive).** For a roughly normal distribution, most values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, the large majority within two, and almost all within three. Stated qualitatively here; the exact figures come later.

## Questions to review

- What does a normal distribution look like, and where is it centered?
- Why do mean and median match for it but not for skewed data?
- How does changing the standard deviation change the curve's shape?
- What does the empirical rule say, in words?
