# Deep Work

## Summary

Newport's case is that long, undistracted concentration on demanding tasks is both increasingly valuable and increasingly rare, which makes the capacity for it a genuine career asset rather than a personality quirk. The first half argues the claim; the second half is the practical part — treating focus as a skill to be trained and scheduled instead of a mood to be awaited. The framing that stuck with me: attention is not spent only by distractions you choose, but by every channel you leave open.

## Key ideas

- Concentration is trainable and degradable — a mind used to switching keeps switching even when you ask it not to.
- Schedule depth rather than hoping for it: decide when deep sessions happen and defend them like meetings.
- Embrace boredom in the gaps; reaching for the phone at every pause trains exactly the wrong reflex.
- Quit or quarantine the tools whose costs outweigh their benefits, instead of keeping everything "just in case".
- Drain the shallows: shallow work expands to fill whatever is undefended.

## Favorite quote

> "Professional activities performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that push your cognitive capabilities to their limit."
> — Cal Newport, defining deep work

## What changed because of it

Two scheduled deep blocks per week became the backbone of my calendar, and the phone now lives in another room during them. Rereading candidate — hence the 5.
