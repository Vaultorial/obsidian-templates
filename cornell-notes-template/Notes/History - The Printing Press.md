# History - The Printing Press

> [!cue] Cues
> - What did Gutenberg actually invent?
> - Why was movable type the leap?
> - Manuscript vs. print — what changed?
> - Effect on literacy & standard language?
> - Why call it a turning point?

> [!notes] Notes
> **Johannes Gutenberg**, in Mainz around the mid-1400s, brought together the pieces of European printing: reusable metal movable type, an oil-based ink that stuck to metal, and an adapted press. The famous Gutenberg Bible came out of this workshop.
>
> **Why movable type mattered.** Earlier book copying was by hand or from carved wood blocks — one whole page per carved block. Movable type meant casting individual letters that could be arranged into a page, printed in quantity, then redistributed to set the next page. Setup was reusable; copying became multiplication rather than transcription.
>
> **Manuscript world vs. print world**
> - Hand-copied books: slow, costly, scarce, and each copy drifted as scribes introduced errors.
> - Printed books: many near-identical copies from one typesetting; cheaper over a run; far more available.
>
> **Downstream effects (over the following decades)**
> - More books at lower cost widened reading beyond clergy and the wealthy.
> - Identical copies meant a shared, fixed reference text — important for scholarship and law.
> - Printing in everyday languages, not only Latin, helped standardize written vernaculars.
>
> Caution from lecture: effects unfolded gradually and unevenly. Not an overnight switch.

> [!summary] Summary
> Gutenberg's mid-15th-century combination of reusable movable metal type, suitable ink and an adapted press turned book production from slow hand-copying into mass reproduction. Cheaper, more plentiful and more uniform books gradually widened literacy, stabilized reference texts and helped standardize vernacular languages — a turning point whose effects accumulated over decades rather than at once.
