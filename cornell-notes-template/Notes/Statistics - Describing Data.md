# Statistics - Describing Data

> [!cue] Cues
> - Why two numbers per dataset?
> - Mean vs. median — when do they split?
> - What does spread add that center misses?
> - Why prefer median for income?
> - Mean = median tells us what?

> [!notes] Notes
> **Two questions for any dataset:** where is the center, and how spread out is it. Center alone hides too much.
>
> **Measures of center**
> - *Mean* — add all values, divide by the count. Uses every value, so a few extreme values drag it.
> - *Median* — the middle value when sorted. Half the data sits on each side; unmoved by extremes.
> - *Mode* — the most frequent value. Mainly for categories.
>
> **Mean vs. median.** On a roughly symmetric set they nearly coincide. When a long tail pulls one way (a few very large values), the mean follows the tail while the median holds near the bulk. Income is the standard case: a handful of very high earners pull the mean above what a typical person earns, so the median describes "typical" more faithfully.
>
> **Measures of spread**
> - *Range* — max minus min. Simple, but set by the two most extreme points.
> - *Standard deviation* — roughly the typical distance of a value from the mean. Small = values cluster; large = values scatter.
>
> Same center, different spread = different stories. Two classes can share an average score while one is uniform and the other splits into high and low groups.

> [!summary] Summary
> Describe data with both a center and a spread. The mean uses every value but is pulled by extremes; the median marks the middle and resists them, so it better describes skewed data like income. Spread (range, standard deviation) says how tightly values cluster — essential context the center alone cannot give.
