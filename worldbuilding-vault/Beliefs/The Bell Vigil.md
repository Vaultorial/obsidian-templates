# The Bell Vigil

Less a religion than a promise kept in public: the sea is owed attention, and the dead it has taken are owed a sound.

## What believers hold

- The sea is not cruel and not kind. It is exact, and it punishes only those who stop listening.
- The drowned are not gone while their bell is still rung. Every tower on [[The Causeway of Bells]] carries the names of those lost near it, and a bell left silent is a second death.

## Practices and holy days

- The Vigil Peal: on the anniversary of [[The Breaking of the Bells]], every tower on the causeway rings the descending peal at the hour the storm struck, and walkers stand still on the stones until it ends.
- Listening at the board: before a crossing, the faithful touch the tide-board and wait for one full bell before stepping out. Visitors from [[Hethwick]] find this slow and charming in equal measure.

## Clergy and authority

- There are no priests. The [[Order of the Drowned Bell]] keeps the name-rolls and casts the bells, and the Warden of the Bells, [[Cole Rowe]], decides when a tower is rung. Most people treat both as the Vigil's keepers without anyone having appointed them.

## Doubts and heresies

- The quiet heresy in [[Lowmark]]: if the sea is exact, the Drownings are not the sea's will but the Court's. The Floodsworn have started to say this out loud.
- Inside the Order, [[Maud Pell]]'s survey raises a harder question. If the charts no longer match the sea, has the Vigil been listening to the wrong thing?

## What it does in the story

- It gives [[The Floodsworn]] their language: "no more drownings" sounds, to any Vigil keeper, like a vow and not a slogan.
- It makes [[The Drowning of Lowmark]] a religious problem as well as a legal one. Flooding a ward on schedule means adding names to a bell on schedule.
