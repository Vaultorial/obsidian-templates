# The Stilt-Folk of Lowmark

"We live where the water lets us." The fishing and moss-grading families of [[Lowmark]], who have built on posts in the bay for as long as anyone has kept records, and who are scheduled, every few years, to lose a ward to the sea.

## Daily life

- The day is set by the tide table, not the sun. Boats go out on the ebb, nets come in before the flood, and the grading-halls work by lamplight when the moss harvest from [[The Mirrormoor]] lands at night.
- Every household keeps a tide-board by the door: a slate where the next two low waters are chalked each morning. Forgetting to update it is a small disgrace.

## Family and kinship

- Houses belong to the post-line, not to a person. A family's posts are handed down to whichever child stays in the bay, and the others are expected to marry onto another line or go up the cliff to [[Ostreve]].
- The old are never left alone on a flood night. Grandparents sleep on the highest floor, and someone young sleeps by the stair.

## Food and drink

- Smoked fish, barley bread bought from [[Hethwick]], and seaweed broth. Pearl-moss is far too valuable to eat; eating it is what people in the bay call a lord's waste.
- At the end of a Drowning, families share a "dry meal" cooked without any water drawn from the bay, to mark the ward they have lost.

## Customs and taboos

- You never whistle on a walkway after dark, because it can cover the sound of the bells from [[The Causeway of Bells]].
- A guest is offered the side of the table facing the water, so they can see the tide coming.

## Names and speech

- Children take the name of their post-line as a surname: Callow, Lowsmith, Rowe. Losing a line in a Drowning means the name has to be carried somewhere new.
- "Low enough" means "safe enough to try". "Above the board" means "none of my business".

## Dress and craft

- Oilskin and undyed wool, soft boots that grip wet timber. Net-menders wear a bone needle on a cord at the throat, and [[Bren Callow]] still does.
- Post-carving is the bay's real art: every load-bearing post is cut with the family's mark and the year it was sunk.

## How outsiders see them

- In [[Ostreve]] they are called the wet ward, and are thought stubborn, superstitious and ungrateful for the dikes. [[The Ebb Court]] sees them as the price of the harbor working.
- The truth the stereotype hides: nobody in the Tide Courts reads the sea better, and the [[Order of the Drowned Bell]] has quietly relied on Lowmark fishermen's reports for generations.
